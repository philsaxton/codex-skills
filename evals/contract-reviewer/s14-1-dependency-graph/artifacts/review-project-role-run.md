## Substantive review

### Architecture and trust boundary

The terminal S2.1 review classified the repeated defect as the same root class: ordinary same-interpreter Python module/class/object behavior could rewrite both stored history and the predicate used to authenticate it. It stopped further patch cycles and required a separately governed architecture decision. The contract responds directly:

- AD-01 rejects same-process memory as the authoritative integrity root without overstating the hostile-runtime threat model.
- AD-02 places lifecycle state and required audit occurrences behind a durable external transaction/authority boundary and defines atomicity, append immutability, ordering, reconstruction, access-control, failure, and recovery predicates.
- AD-03 bounds the claim to ordinary application-process mutation, application bugs, and supported runtime interfaces, while reserving privileged administrator/root/operator threats to later operational work.
- AD-04 preserves the exact S1.1 wire and lifecycle invariants and S1.2 TM-015/TC-12 fail-closed objective.
- AD-05 salvages useful profile-content and deterministic-domain work only as non-authoritative reference material subject to a fresh contract and re-verification.

The contract does not mistake a hash chain, private name, slot, immutable tuple, defensive copy, or second in-memory oracle for an authority boundary. It also does not require resistance to a compromised database administrator, infrastructure root, interpreter, or storage operator. That is the correct minimum correction under the historical authorization.

### Product and security preservation

The MVP proposal already requires immutable profile history, authorized activation/rollback, audit events, deterministic server-side profile selection, tenant isolation, human review, no autonomous approval, and no send. The correction changes no product outcome and requires no proposal amendment.

S1.1 remains the controlling schema/interface source. The contract preserves `Profile`, `ProfileVersion`, and `AuditEvent` shapes; stable identity; copy-on-write content; at-most-one-active tuple; explicit rollback; first-transition timestamps; actor/tenant/scope provenance; append-only events; atomicity; minimization; and no-send/no-approval boundaries.

S1.2 explicitly assigns TM-015/TC-12 audit omission, mutation, ordering, occurrence identity, authority binding, and alertability controls, while recognizing that durable persistence/transactions, durable audit controls, retention, backup/restore, privileged operations, and deployment evidence belong to later gates. The contract preserves that split: S2.3 owns the minimum durable lifecycle/audit authority; S7.2 retains privileged operations, monitoring, retention, backup, restore, incident, and operational-audit ownership.

### Backlog and interface reconciliation

The baseline scheduling graph makes S2.2 depend directly on S1.2 and S2.1, S3.3 and S6.1 depend on S2.2, and S5.1 depend directly on S2.1 and S4.2. That graph would allow a reference-only S2.1 to unlock runtime profile use.

The contract repairs that graph without a hidden prose gate:

- revised S2.1 owns closed profile content, canonicalization, copy-on-write versions, deterministic lifecycle rules, and reference/test-double behavior, with no production or tamper-resistant authority claim;
- new P0 executable S2.3 owns durable authoritative lifecycle and audit integrity and depends on S1.2 plus revised S2.1;
- S2.2 directly adds S2.3, so S3.3 and S6.1 inherit the authority gate through their existing S2.2 dependency;
- S5.1 replaces S2.1 with S2.3 while retaining S4.2, closing the outreach-profile bypass;
- E2 cannot complete until revised S2.1, S2.2, and S2.3 are complete or separately and validly deferred; and
- no downstream slice is promoted.

The resulting graph has resolved dependencies, no self-edge, and no cycle. The future S2.3 interface obligations fix decision-bearing semantics—server-derived command authority, canonical transaction result, authoritative reads, history immutability, global ordering, fail-closed behavior, security separation, and recovery—while correctly leaving vendor, physical schema, migration, service topology, operator-role, retention, and deployment choices to fresh executable/operations contracts.
